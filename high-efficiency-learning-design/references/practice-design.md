# Practice Design Reference

Read this file when constructing lessons, exercises, tutoring interactions, worked examples, practice sets, cumulative assessments, or when deciding how much guidance to provide.


## P1 — Active performance is the learning engine

Passive consumption can orient a learner, but do not treat understanding while watching or reading as evidence of learning. The operational test is whether the learner can later reproduce or use the knowledge independently.

Default lesson rhythm:

1. Deliver a **minimum effective dose** of explanation.
2. Show one worked example or demonstration when the procedure is novel.
3. Require an independent attempt almost immediately.
4. Provide corrective feedback tied to the learner's actual error.
5. Repeat with small variations.
6. Increase difficulty only after success becomes reliable.

Keep the ratio of learner performance to passive explanation high. If an explanation is long, break it with retrieval or application rather than asking the learner to consume the entire explanation first.

## P2 — Active does not mean unguided

For novice acquisition, prefer explicit guidance plus active performance. Do not infer from “active learning” that the learner should discover the method from scratch, work in a group, or solve an open-ended project before possessing the component schemas.

Use the following default hierarchy for novice skill acquisition:

**active + direct guidance > active + minimal guidance > passive + direct explanation**

The exact ordering is stated in an unfinished direct-instruction chapter but is supported elsewhere in the finished chapter on effective practice. Treat the qualitative rule as strong; do not present the displayed inequality as an experimentally universal law across every domain.

Use discovery or open-ended problem solving when:

- the learner already has the relevant schemas;
- generation/creativity is itself the target;
- the task is representative of expert performance;
- failure will reveal useful information rather than merely produce confusion.

## P3 — Deliberate practice requires both mindfulness and repetition

A task qualifies as good deliberate practice when it has all of these properties:

- it targets a specific performance weakness or next-edge skill;
- it is just beyond current reliable performance but still achievable;
- the learner is concentrating rather than executing automatically without thought;
- feedback arrives soon enough to inform the next attempt;
- the learner can make a concrete adjustment after the feedback;
- the cycle repeats enough times for small adjustments to compound.

Reject two common impostors:

### Mindless repetition

The learner repeats a comfortable action without diagnosing or changing anything. This stabilizes current performance more than it extends capability.

### Low-cycle “challenge” practice

The learner spends a long time on one problem so far outside their repertoire that very few action-feedback-adjustment cycles occur. The task may be interesting enrichment, but it is a poor default for efficient skill acquisition.

When forced to choose, prefer many short, targeted, progressively harder cycles over one heroic struggle, provided the short cycles genuinely stretch performance.

## P4 — Difficulty must be desirable, not merely difficult

Treat difficulty as useful when it forces retrieval, discrimination, reconstruction, or refined execution **and the learner can succeed**.

Signs of desirable difficulty:

- retrieval feels effortful after a delay but succeeds;
- mixed practice lowers worksheet fluency yet improves method selection;
- a learner initially doubts a task but completes it after focused effort;
- a task exposes a weakness that can be corrected on the next attempt.

Signs of undesirable difficulty:

- repeated failure without progress;
- confusion caused by an unmastered prerequisite;
- too many novel elements must be held simultaneously;
- a timed condition is added before accurate untimed performance exists;
- a challenge consumes practice time without producing actionable feedback.

Never defend poor instructional design by calling frustration a “desirable difficulty.”

## P5 — Design the learning staircase

When a learner cannot complete a task, first ask whether the step is too large.

Break a complex skill into successively harder **knowledge units**. A unit should normally introduce one meaningful new demand while keeping prerequisite demands familiar enough to leave working-memory capacity for the new material.

Within a unit:

1. State or demonstrate the key subgoal.
2. Show a worked example if the learner lacks a schema.
3. Give a closely aligned problem.
4. Add one source of variation at a time.
5. Combine components only after the parts are stable enough.

Use subgoal labels to make structural similarity visible. Use diagrams or visual representations when they clarify structure rather than decorate the lesson.

## P6 — Externalize working memory before blaming understanding

If the learner can perform components separately but fails when they are combined, reduce working-memory burden.

Have the learner write intermediate values, assumptions, subgoals, or partial results when either condition holds:

1. doing the step mentally is not highly reliable and low-effort; or
2. the learner must juggle several pieces rather than manipulate one cohesive chunk.

The trade-off is asymmetric. Writing an unnecessary intermediate step usually costs seconds; omitting a needed step can cause cascading errors or complete overload. When uncertain, externalize a little more, then fade the support as the task becomes one coherent chunk.

This applies beyond paper: scratchpads, diagrams, state tables, debugger watches, checklists, and intermediate artifacts can all serve as external memory.

## P7 — Baseline mastery and automaticity are different thresholds

Do not keep a learner on one skill until execution is completely automatic before allowing progress.

Use two thresholds:

- **Baseline mastery:** performance is correct and reliable enough that advanced learning can safely build on it.
- **Automaticity:** execution becomes fast and low-effort enough that it consumes little conscious working memory.

Advance after baseline mastery, then continue building automaticity through spaced retrieval and layering. This avoids both errors:

- moving on while the foundation is too shaky to support anything;
- overlearning one isolated skill long after additional blocked practice has diminishing returns.

Check automaticity later through cumulative or appropriately timed performance, especially for foundational skills that are repeatedly reused.

## P8 — Automaticity enables, rather than opposes, higher-order thinking

When a complex task requires many component operations, weak automaticity forces the learner to spend working memory on low-level mechanics. That leaves less capacity for planning, interpretation, abstraction, creativity, and error monitoring.

If a learner understands a high-level concept verbally but repeatedly fails its execution, inspect component fluency before adding more conceptual discussion.

Conversely, do not reduce automaticity training to blind speed drills. Accuracy and correct procedure come first. Speed matters only insofar as low-level execution must become cognitively cheap for the higher-level target.

## P9 — Fade scaffolding as expertise grows

Support is stage-dependent.

For novices:

- worked examples;
- explicit steps;
- close problem-example similarity;
- immediate corrective feedback;
- small increments in difficulty.

For intermediate learners:

- fewer cues;
- mixed examples;
- delayed reference access;
- larger combinations of known components;
- transfer questions.

For experts:

- open-ended problems;
- sparse or no worked examples;
- self-diagnosis;
- creative generation;
- realistic performance constraints.

Do not keep novice scaffolds forever. A scaffold becomes a crutch when the learner succeeds only because the cue remains present.

## P10 — Use reference material like a spotter, not a lifting machine

Reference-use protocol:

1. Attempt independently first.
2. If progress is genuinely blocked, identify the smallest missing cue.
3. Peek at only that cue or a corresponding worked-example step.
4. Close the reference.
5. Reconstruct the step from memory.
6. Finish independently if possible.

If the learner solves alongside the reference, do not count the performance as independent retrieval or mastery evidence.

Notes can be useful for attention and organizing new information, but beware notes designed primarily to prevent future retrieval. If the learner continually consults excellent notes, the notes may hide weak memory rather than strengthen it.

## P11 — Group output and individual learning are different objectives

A professional team divides labor to maximize total output. A learning activity must ensure each learner performs the target skills.

Use group work when social coordination, communication, or collaborative performance is itself the target, or when it improves motivation without replacing core practice.

If the goal is individual mastery, structure group activity so that every learner still executes each target component. Do not assume that observing a teammate's part produces equivalent learning.

## P12 — Concepts and procedures should reinforce each other

Avoid false sequencing rules such as “teach every concept before any procedure” or “drill procedures without meaning.” In hierarchical domains, the relationship is recursive:

- simple concepts support procedures;
- executing procedures creates concrete experience;
- that experience supports higher-level concepts and abstractions;
- higher-level concepts organize and improve later procedures.

For abstract material, build a sufficient base of concrete examples and procedural experience before expecting a learner to appreciate the abstraction fully.

## P13 — Time pressure comes after accuracy

Use timed retrieval only when speed or automaticity is relevant to the real target and the learner already demonstrates accurate untimed performance.

Good uses:

- checking whether foundational operations are becoming automatic;
- rehearsing an exam or professional constraint that is genuinely timed;
- cumulative sampling of well-learned material.

Bad uses:

- teaching a novel procedure;
- forcing a weak learner to trade accuracy for speed;
- introducing anxiety without a performance reason;
- timing tasks whose complexity makes fast response irrelevant.

A timed assessment should still diagnose learning, not merely create pressure.

## Practice-design audit checklist

When reviewing an existing lesson or study method, ask:

- Does the learner perform something within minutes of receiving instruction?
- Does each exercise target a defined capability or weakness?
- Is the next step challenging but realistically achievable?
- Is feedback specific enough to change the next attempt?
- Are there enough cycles for adjustments to compound?
- Is blocked repetition stopped once baseline mastery is reached?
- Is working memory overloaded by avoidable bookkeeping?
- Are worked examples being faded as the learner grows?
- Can the learner perform without notes, hints, or the recent lesson context?
- Are low-level skills reliable enough to support the higher-level target?
