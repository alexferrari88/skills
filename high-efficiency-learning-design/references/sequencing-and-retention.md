# Sequencing and Retention Reference

Read this file when designing a curriculum, deciding what to learn next, scheduling review, mixing topics, planning exam preparation, or deciding whether a learner is ready to accelerate.


## S1 — Sequence by prerequisites, not calendar or age

In a hierarchical domain, a learner is ready for a new skill when the prerequisites needed to learn that skill have been demonstrated to a sufficient baseline mastery level.

Operationally:

1. Define the target skill.
2. Identify direct prerequisites actually used by the target.
3. Verify the learner's performance on those prerequisites.
4. Offer target skills whose prerequisites are all sufficiently mastered.
5. Let different learners require different amounts of practice and progress at different rates.

Do not hold a learner on already-mastered material merely to preserve lockstep pacing. Conversely, do not push a learner into advanced material because of age, schedule, or prior course completion if required prerequisites are weak.

For real-world grade skipping or placement, prerequisite mastery answers the **content-readiness** question. Social arrangements, institutional rules, workload, and the learner's goals are separate decision variables. That separation is a derived application boundary rather than a direct claim from the book.

## S2 — Use the knowledge frontier as the default source of new tasks

The learner's **knowledge frontier** is the boundary between demonstrated knowledge and not-yet-learned material. Frontier tasks are unmastered skills whose prerequisites are mastered.

When several frontier tasks are available, prefer tasks that:

- advance the user's stated goal;
- unlock many important downstream skills;
- reuse and reinforce recently mastered foundations;
- maintain enough diversity to avoid long runs of confusable material;
- can be learned with the available tools and time.

If a learner repeatedly fails one frontier branch, temporarily advance along another independent branch rather than making all progress contingent on one blockage.

## S3 — Prioritize core topics without confusing priority with completeness

A **core** skill has high downstream relevance: many later skills depend on it, directly or indirectly.

For a goal-directed program:

1. Trace prerequisites backward from the goal.
2. Rank prerequisites by downstream unlock value and frequency of reuse.
3. Teach high-leverage prerequisites early so later tasks repeatedly layer on them.
4. Defer material unrelated to the goal unless comprehensive coverage is itself required.

This is not permission to omit supplemental topics from a course that promises comprehensive mastery. It is a sequencing and scope rule.

## S4 — Layer immediately after baseline mastery

Once a prerequisite reaches baseline mastery, use new skills that build on it rather than continuing isolated overlearning indefinitely.

Layering gives two benefits:

- the advanced task reinforces the prerequisite through use;
- the strengthened prerequisite makes the advanced task easier to acquire.

When a learner says an earlier topic still does not feel maximally intuitive despite passing it, do not automatically hold them back. If execution is reliable, forward progress plus later review may deepen understanding more efficiently than more same-context repetition.

## S5 — Distinguish four scheduling strategies

| Strategy | Main problem solved | Best stage | Main failure if misused |
|---|---|---|---|
| Initial blocking | Learn how to execute one new method | Initial acquisition | Overlearning creates fluency without durable discrimination |
| Non-interference | Prevent newly learned similar associations from colliding | Fragile acquisition | Excessive separation can prevent later discrimination practice |
| Interleaving | Learn to choose among methods/categories | After baseline mastery | Too early can overload a learner who cannot execute the methods yet |
| Spacing | Preserve and strengthen memory over time | After initial learning | Too early wastes review; too late causes failed retrieval/relearning |

### Derived reconciliation: non-interference vs interleaving

The book argues both that confusable new concepts should be separated and that mixed practice improves discrimination. Apply them at different stages:

1. During fragile initial encoding, avoid teaching highly confusable novel associations in immediate succession if interference is likely.
2. Once each concept has baseline mastery, deliberately mix them so the learner must discriminate between them.

This preserves the distinctive insight of both chapters without turning either into an absolute rule.

## S6 — Stop blocked overlearning after the minimum effective dose

A small block of same-type problems can be useful immediately after instruction because the learner needs to practice execution before also carrying the burden of method selection.

Once the learner reaches reliable performance, additional immediate repetitions have diminishing long-term value. Redirect that time to:

- a different new frontier skill;
- a due review;
- a previously learned contrasting problem type;
- a higher-level task that layers on the skill.

Do not infer long-term mastery from rapid improvement inside a homogeneous problem block.

## S7 — Interleave to train discrimination, not just variety

Interleaving should force a real choice. A mixed worksheet where every problem labels the method (“use the chain rule”) does not fully exercise discrimination.

For each review item, remove cues that would not exist in the target environment and ask the learner to determine:

- what type of problem this is;
- what information matters;
- which technique applies;
- why a tempting alternative does not apply.

Mix superficially similar problems that require different techniques once those techniques are individually learned. This is especially valuable for word problems and cumulative assessments.

## S8 — Space review until retrieval is effortful but likely to succeed

Use performance-responsive intervals rather than a fixed universal schedule.

Basic update rule:

1. After initial mastery, wait long enough for some retrieval effort to return.
2. On successful independent retrieval, lengthen the next interval.
3. On slow, error-prone, or failed retrieval, shorten the interval and inspect prerequisites.
4. Continue expanding intervals as memory becomes more durable.

The objective is not continuous freshness. A review that is trivially easy because the answer is still in working memory provides less consolidation than one requiring successful reconstruction from long-term memory.

Do not prescribe exact interval multipliers from this skill. The book emphasizes calibration to learner and topic; its production system uses proprietary adaptive logic.

## S9 — Combine spacing with retrieval

When review time arrives, test rather than merely restudy.

Preferred review:

1. present a problem, question, or production task without the answer;
2. require an independent attempt;
3. give corrective feedback;
4. if the learner had to consult a reference, close it and retrieve again before crediting the repetition fully.

A review session spent rereading can refresh familiarity without producing the same retrieval practice.

## S10 — Count implicit review only when the prerequisite is actually exercised

An advanced task can reinforce simpler skills, but distinguish **prerequisite** from **encompassed practice**.

Example pattern:

- Advanced skill A requires conceptual knowledge of B but does not make the learner execute B -> do **not** treat A as a full repetition of B.
- Advanced skill A repeatedly executes B as a component -> A can count as implicit practice of B.
- A uses only a subset or easier case of B -> at most partial credit is justified.

When uncertain, prefer explicit review. The source's adaptive system uses fractional implicit credit; manual learning plans do not need to imitate its numeric machinery.

## S11 — Use broad cumulative assessment to reveal hidden fragility

A learner can perform well in a lesson because the method is primed by the surrounding context. To assess durable mastery:

- sample older as well as recent material;
- mix problem types;
- avoid announcing the exact technique needed;
- remove references for the assessment;
- use time constraints only if automaticity/speed matters and untimed accuracy already exists.

If lesson performance is strong but cumulative performance is weak, diagnose retrieval, discrimination, spacing, or automaticity before adding another explanation of the concept.

## S12 — Switch into freshness mode for a near-term exam

Long-run spacing aims to minimize review while preventing destructive forgetting. A near-term high-stakes exam changes the objective: the learner temporarily wants a broad body of knowledge unusually fresh at one date.

In test-prep mode:

1. increase review density across the tested domain;
2. use representative, timed practice tests only after course knowledge is sufficiently built;
3. analyze errors by underlying skill and remediate those skills rather than only taking more full tests;
4. practice the actual distribution of item difficulty and time pressure;
5. continue retrieval rather than passive cramming.

Do not generalize the book's platform-specific “six weeks/six tests” rule to every exam. Treat it as one product experience, not a universal constant.

## S13 — Prefer frequent, sustainable sessions to sparse marathons

For the same weekly volume, shorter and more frequent sessions usually improve habit consistency, reduce fatigue, and create more natural spacing.

Do not make sessions so short that setup/context-switching dominates. The source gives roughly 20 minutes as a practical lower-bound example, but this is a rule of thumb rather than a cognitive constant.

Increase session length only while productive attention and accuracy remain high. If long sessions cause zoning out, error spikes, or skipped subsequent days, redistribute the volume.

## S14 — Acceleration is a consequence of efficiency, not skipped content

When a learner masters prerequisites rapidly, allow forward progress. “Acceleration” should mean the learner covers more properly mastered material per unit calendar time, not that content or mastery criteria are removed.

Audit acceleration with three checks:

1. Was prerequisite content actually demonstrated?
2. Does cumulative performance show retention rather than recent priming?
3. Can the learner execute the foundations reliably enough that advanced tasks do not overload them?

If these hold, being ahead of conventional age/grade pace is not itself a reason to slow down.

## S15 — Match curriculum granularity and review density to the learner

A favorable learning environment does not give everyone identical tiny steps. Excessive scaffolding can throttle a fast learner just as insufficient scaffolding can overload a slower learner.

Adapt two quantities:

- **bite size:** how much novelty is introduced per instructional step;
- **review density:** how much reinforcement is needed before and after advancement.

Fast, robust learner -> larger bites and less frequent review.

Slower or more forgetful learner -> smaller bites and more explicit review.

Keep the mastery standard stable while changing how much support/practice is required to meet it.
